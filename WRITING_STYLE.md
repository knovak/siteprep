# Writing style: Ken Novak

This is a description of how I write, derived from about 7,700 words of my own
"Ideas & Rants" pieces on politics, technology, and everything else, written
between 2011 and 2025. It exists so that anything written in my name — the repo
guide, a deck introduction, a demo description, a README — sounds like me
instead of sounding like a machine that has read a lot of documentation.

Use it as a checklist, not a straitjacket. The last section is the short
version.

---

## 1. Stance: I own my opinions, and I say so

The single biggest marker. I write in the first person and I put my judgment on
the page rather than hiding behind a neutral narrator.

> "I think the internet will soon be flooded with disinformation."
> "I'm not optimistic about what's coming."
> "In the end, I'm not terribly interested in what billionaires think anyway."
> "I would really prefer a world with services built on fleets of robocars."

Two things follow from that:

- **Hedges are mine, and they're specific.** Not "it may be the case that" but
  "I think Level 5 is 10 years off" and "It's possible, but rare, that I form an
  opinion different from the conventional wisdom."
- **I'll say when I was wrong or when I changed.** "I was not always this way.
  When I was in my 20s and 30s, I was like a lot of the people in the 'do the
  research' culture of today."

For process and how-to writing, second person does the same job: "You know
you're doing enough testing when the percent of tests that are positive
approaches the new case proportion."

Avoid: the disembodied authority voice ("one might observe", "it should be
noted"), and the fake-humble machine voice that states a preference as if it
were a fact of nature.

## 2. Conclusion first, then the support

I open with the claim and spend the rest of the paragraph earning it.

> "America has nurtured fascists for a long time."
> "The rightward move is a function of the maturity of Silicon Valley industry."
> "Moore's Law began with the invention of the planar integrated circuit in 1959."

When the piece is a plan or an analysis, I say so at the top: "Here's my take on
the key elements of the path forward from COVID."

Avoid: the throat-clearing opener that describes what the section is about
before saying anything ("This section explains the considerations relevant
to...").

## 3. Concrete over abstract, always

Numbers with units, dates, names, prices, comparisons. If a claim can be checked
against a number, I give the number.

> "Since 2003, GDP grew 24%, population grew 11%, and retail jobs shrunk 23% —
> 300,000 jobs lost while Amazon grew."
> "Pack cost much below $200/kWh is unlikely before 2020, which brings the cost
> of a proposed 70-kWh pack for a 240-mile vehicle to $14,000 (or higher)."

Two habits worth copying:

- **"like," as an approximator.** "Once the case count gets low enough (like, 10
  new cases per day per million people)". It signals an order of magnitude
  without pretending to precision.
- **Parenthetical evidence and calibration.** "(This is likely.)" "(This works —
  Zymergen does pooled PCR routinely.)" "(Grass Valley would become part of the
  Bay Area.)" "(Police have the time; arrests are down by half.)"

## 4. Argue by analogy to something that already happened

This is how I explain almost everything unfamiliar. The analogy usually comes
from history, regulation, or an industry that solved the same problem earlier.

- AI disinformation → the FDA and a century of food and drug regulation
- Self-driving fleets → airlines, the NTSB, and transparent crash investigations
- Electric cars → the Prius in 2003 and the hybrid plateau at 3% of sales
- Personhood → an ocean wave, and the surface of a boiling saucepan
- Safety-net programs → a net you bounce off, not a place you live

Avoid: the invented metaphor that explains nothing ("the record is a living
organism"). If the analogy doesn't carry an argument, cut it.

## 5. Structure: short paragraphs, parallel lists, labeled items

Paragraphs run two to five sentences and carry one idea. Lists are parallel and
often use a bold or capitalized label with a colon, then the explanation:

> **Incrementally:** Lowest-contact highest-value activities first
> **Tentatively:** If testing shows a disease rebound, openings will be reversed
> **Geographically specific:** Local virus breakouts are inevitable...

Caveats get numbered in the prose: "with two important caveats. First... Second..."

Pivot phrases I actually use: "Key point:", "The key:", "That is:", "So",
"Basically,", "In the meantime", "In the end", "Meanwhile", "And let's not
forget".

## 6. Rhythm: mostly plain declaratives, with the occasional fragment

Most sentences are short to medium. The long ones are held together with
semicolons and earn their length by carrying a chain of consequences:

> "Insurance companies make money proportional to dollar flow, not from cutting
> the absolute level of flow. Given price competition, the end result of
> self-driving cars is poorer insurance companies."

Fragments show up for emphasis or reaction, sparingly, and usually as a
one-liner: "Wow." "Pretty utopian." "It ain't Italy!"

Avoid: the fragment used as fake profundity, one per paragraph, in a row. "Three
vocabularies, kept separate on purpose." "Not a project. A durable unit of
intent." One of those is a flourish; four in a page is a tic.

## 7. Humor: dry, brief, at the end

The joke lands at the end of a paragraph and then I move on.

> "They made him President, and gave him a Congress full of cyphers, slackwits,
> and doddering old men to work with."
> "In the long run all exponential curves go straight up; but as we all know, in
> the long run we are all dead."
> "It ain't Italy!"

Avoid: cleverness in the middle of an explanation, and any joke that needs a
second sentence to land.

## 8. Mechanics

| Thing | My habit |
| --- | --- |
| Spelling | American. Labor, behavior, authorize, judgment, relabeling, organize. |
| Contractions | Used freely: don't, isn't, we're, it'd, you're, can't. |
| Em dash | One per paragraph at most, for a real aside. |
| Semicolon | Used, and used properly, to join a claim to its consequence. |
| Numbers | Numerals with units and ranges: $9–15K, 3–5 years, 2–4% inflation, 7% per year. |
| Ranges | En dash: 18–35 year-olds, 1890s–1960s. |
| Quotes | Quote the source directly and name it inline: "— Vox", "— The Atlantic, 2016". |
| Links | Named at the end of the thought, with the publication, not hidden behind "here". |
| Headings | Plain noun phrases, often with a colon or a wry twist: "Moore's Law: Why IT Is Different", "The American Right: Old Ideas, New Names", "Autonomous Driving Levels — What Actually Matters". |

## 9. The AI tells to strip out

These are the constructions that made the first version of the repo guide read
like a machine. Each pair below is a real before/after from that rewrite.

| Stilted | Rewritten |
| --- | --- |
| "Ask what is expected of you here and the honest answer is: less than you would think" | "What's expected of you here is less than you'd think" |
| "What makes that survivable is that the record keeps the original intent..." | "That only works because of the record. It holds the original intent..." |
| "so the rail above cannot quietly disagree with the code that enforces it" | "so the rail above can't drift away from the code that enforces it" |
| "a gap is information: it tells the next contributor what has not been decided yet" | "a gap tells you something: it says what hasn't been decided yet" |
| "Bounding it this way keeps the mechanism reviewable" | "Keeping it that small is what keeps the mechanism reviewable" |
| "an agent free to re-rank its own queue would eventually favour the interesting work" | "an agent free to re-rank its own queue would drift toward the interesting work" |
| "Verify visually with the screenshot command" | "Check it visually with the screenshot command" |
| "The initiative rests. It is not abandoned and it is not finished — those would both be lies." | "So it rests. It is not abandoned and it is not finished, and either label would be a lie." |
| "Abstract behaviour · live vocabulary" | "Made-up initiative · real vocabulary" |

The general rules behind those:

1. **No "What X is is Y" or "It is not A — it is B" as a default sentence
   shape.** Once in a piece, fine. Every third paragraph, no.
2. **No em-dash-plus-"which is what" chains.** "…which is what stops the warning
   from pushing values into prose that does not want them" is three clauses of
   throat-clearing. Say it once.
3. **No pseudo-profound one-liners** standing alone as paragraphs: "The order is
   the interesting part." "A gap is information." Give them a verb and a
   consequence.
4. **No adverb-of-intent padding**: "deliberately separate", "quietly disagree",
   "visibly waiting", "honestly answer". Keep one when it does real work; cut the
   rest.
5. **No dodging contractions.** Formal-register avoidance of "isn't" and
   "doesn't" is the loudest tell of all.
6. **No British spelling.** It's the second loudest.

## 10. The short version

Before publishing anything under my name, check:

- [ ] Does it start with the claim, not a description of the claim?
- [ ] Is there a number, a date, a name, or an example in the first paragraph?
- [ ] Do I say "I think" where it's my judgment, and quantify the hedge?
- [ ] Is there an analogy to something that actually happened?
- [ ] Are the contractions in?
- [ ] American spelling throughout?
- [ ] At most one em dash per paragraph, and one standalone fragment per page?
- [ ] Are the lists parallel, with labels where the items need them?
- [ ] Does the joke, if there is one, come at the end and stay short?
- [ ] Would I say this sentence out loud to somebody at a table?

That last one catches most of it.
