The shortage is permits, not money

Matthew Y — 26 January 2026

The argument I want to make is narrow: the shortage everyone is describing is a shortage of permits, not of money. That distinction matters because the two have opposite remedies, and for four years we have been applying the second to the first.

Start with the money. Capital committed to the sector has risen every year since 2019 (study 1), and the cost of that capital fell through 2024 (study 2). Funds report difficulty deploying rather than raising (study 3, study 4). If money were binding we would see the opposite of all three.

Now the permits. Median time from application to decision has roughly doubled (study 5), and the variance has grown faster than the median (study 6) — which is worse than it sounds, because a project cannot be financed against a distribution that wide (study 7, study 8).

The obvious objection is that permits are slow because there are more applications. That is true (study 9) and it does not explain the variance (study 10), nor the fact that the slowest jurisdictions are not the busiest ones (study 11, study 12).

A second objection: this is a staffing problem, and staffing is money. Partly (study 13). But two states doubled their permitting staff and saw median times fall by under a tenth (study 14, study 15), because the binding step was sequential review rather than headcount (study 16).

What would change my mind is evidence that the sequencing reforms of the last eighteen months (study 17, study 18) have not moved the median. So far they have (study 19), including in the two jurisdictions that made no other change (study 20, study 21).

The policy conclusion is unglamorous and I think it is right: sequence the reviews in parallel, publish the queue, and stop announcing money (study 22, study 23). Everything else follows from the queue being visible (study 24), which is the one thing nobody has been willing to do (study 25).
