Energy Notes — January 2026

Eight things worth reading this month, with a note on each.

Grid interconnection queues are the bottleneck

The queue to connect a project to the grid now runs longer than the time it takes to build one, and the papers this month put numbers on how much of the delay is study work that could be done in parallel.

Industrial heat is the hard part

Electrifying process heat above four hundred degrees remains the open problem, and this review is the clearest account of which processes are close and which are not.

What the storage build actually displaced

A careful counterfactual analysis of two years of battery deployment.

The headline result is that storage displaced peaking gas rather than baseload, which is what the modelling predicted and had not been measured at scale.

The second result is less comfortable: in one market the batteries raised emissions slightly for six months, because they charged on the margin from coal and discharged against gas.

Transmission cost overruns, catalogued

A database of ninety transmission projects and what each one ended up costing against its first estimate, which is the kind of unglamorous work the field has been missing.

Demand response finally has a price

Two markets ran auctions that let demand response bid against generation on equal terms, and the clearing prices are the first honest estimate of what flexibility is worth.

Heat pump performance in cold climates

Field data from four thousand installations north of the fiftieth parallel, which puts a long-running argument about coefficient of performance in real winters to rest.

The permitting reform nobody noticed

A procedural change to how environmental reviews are sequenced has cut median review time by a third, with no measurable change in outcomes.

Electricity demand is growing again

After fifteen flat years, demand is rising, and the paper separates how much is data centres, how much is electrification, and how much is weather.
