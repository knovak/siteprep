2026-11-15 — Upgrade the beta in five units, from a comparison with another simulator

The user supplies "Visual Comparison of Two Orbital Data Center Sky Simulators", 16 paired scenarios captured on 12 October 2026, and asks: "Plan a set of upgrades to the beta version of SBDC. Study this doc, and compare the code and the display to https://github.com/sdross0/orbital-datacenter-brightness. Correct any bugs you discover in SBDC." The features asked for are presets that correspond to that project's default setups, an option to include moonlight and its effects, an approximate ratio of SBDC light to the Moon's, an option to simulate ambient light, and "perhaps" the number of hours in a night or a year in which SBDC light is significant at a location, plus any improvement the document suggests.

The user adds: "Consider the testing strategy when make the plan, to streamline the process. Break the entire plan into 5 units of work, and immediately start to execute the first unit. Each unit should involve deployment to beta, and a corresponding PR." A later message in the same session asks that the decisions made and the plan to be executed be documented.

Decided: the work is five units, each one pull request, in this order. The full plan, the fault list and the testing strategy are in beta/PLAN.md.

UnitContent
1The eight faults that do not change the model's numbers, and the checks the later units reuse
2Constellation and brightness settings that let the sdross0 setups be reproduced, presets for them, and a link that carries the settings
3The twilight sky-brightness correction, and ambient light by Bortle class
4Moonlight, and SBDC light compared with the Moon
5Hours of significant SBDC light per night and per year, a perspective view, and aiming at the densest sky

What was decided, and why

The plan is in beta/PLAN.md. plan.md and spec.md are the supplied July 2026 documents, byte for byte, and adoption-manifest.json records them as such. A beta plan appended to plan.md would break that record.

The initiative stays dormant, and the units are not todo items. The work is done in the session that was asked for it. An actionable todo item in a non-dormant initiative is work the scheduled sweep may pick up, so listing the units there would let a sweep run start a unit the session is already in. This follows the 2026-11-11 settings work, which was also done at the user's request with the stage unchanged. release-after-testing and mobile-sky-follow-up keep their blockers.

Each unit's pull request is merged when the site workflow has passed. The beta is published by the site build from main, so a merge is the only way a unit reaches the beta, and the user asked that each unit include that deployment. The units also build on each other, and the session has one branch, so unit 2 cannot be opened until unit 1 has merged. Nothing here releases the simulator: lib/, work/ and demos/SBDC Night Sky/ are not changed, and the 2026-09-11 instruction "no additional releases" still governs them.

The other project's code is read, not copied and not run. The checks use the numbers it has published: the 16 whole-sky counts in the comparison document and the tables in its README. The formulas this simulator needs from it are from the papers it cites (Boley, Lawler and Rein 2026; Mallama et al. 2023; Patat et al. 2006; Schaefer 1990), written here from those descriptions.

Every new model setting defaults to the current behaviour. A person who opens the beta and changes nothing sees the same constellation and the same brightness as before. The two fixture files are the check: a unit that adds a setting may not change test/golden/golden.json or the Site 1 column of test/fixtures/paired-comparisons.json.

The twilight table is the one exception, because it is a fault. skyBrightness.table in sbdc-data.json cites Patat et al. (2006) and gives 16.5 mag/arcsec² at the zenith with the Sun 6° below the horizon. That paper's V-band fit gives 13.30 there, 17.00 at 9° and 19.67 at 12°; the table has 16.5, 18.2 and 19.5. The sky at the end of civil twilight is therefore drawn about 19 times darker than measured, and stars and satellites are counted as visible too early. Unit 3 corrects the table. This lowers the counts at dusk and dawn, which includes the opening view and the three existing presets, all 30 to 60 minutes from sunset or sunrise. The fixtures are regenerated in that pull request and the change is listed there.

The naked-eye threshold table is left as it is. The other project uses a closed form from Schaefer (1990), which is stricter under a bright sky: with the Sun 6° down it gives a limit near magnitude -0.6, where this simulator's table, once the sky is corrected, gives about 1.5. The brightest stars are in practice visible at the end of civil twilight, which the table matches more closely. The two limits agree to about 0.2 magnitude once the sky is dark.

"In view" and "whole sky" are separate numbers in the readout. The document compares whole-sky totals and notes that the other site also prints a smaller count for its picture. The readout now gives both in the horizon view.

Ambient light is a second control beside the eye limit. The existing "Eye / sky quality limit" is a cap on the faintest magnitude seen. Ambient light is a sky brightness, by Bortle class, added to the twilight sky. They are different things, so both stay and the first is relabelled.

The Moon needs a year, and is placed for longitude 0. The simulator has a latitude, a date and a local solar time, and no longitude. The Moon moves about half a degree an hour, so taking the solar time as universal time places it within about 7° for any longitude. A phase can also be set by hand, which needs no date.

"Significant" is reported under three definitions. The request does not define it. Unit 5 reports the hours for each of: at least 100 SBDCs visible to the naked eye; visible SBDCs outnumber visible stars; SBDC light exceeds the natural moonless night sky. All three include moonlight and ambient light when those are on.

2026-11-15 — Beta upgrade, unit 3: a threshold option, the color ramp, and the opening view

Three things came up while unit 3 was done that the entry above did not settle. They are recorded here because one of them revises it.

The Schaefer threshold becomes an option. The entry above left the naked-eye threshold table as it was and listed the Schaefer closed form as an alternative not taken. Correcting the twilight sky was expected to bring this simulator's twilight counts close to the other simulator's. It moved the optimistic-case ratio at 55° N only from 3.9 to 3.3. The larger part of the difference is the threshold relation, which the other simulator takes from Schaefer (1990): with the Sun 10° down it gives a limit about a magnitude lower than the table. The table stays the default, because with the corrected sky it matches when stars are seen to appear in twilight. The closed form is added as advanced.naked_eye_threshold: schaefer, and the three sdross0 presets use it, since those presets exist to correspond to that project's setups. With it the same ratio is 1.8, and the no-mitigation ratios in twilight are 1.00 and 1.04.

The sky color ramp's anchors moved with the table. The ramp maps sky brightness to color, with anchors that were values of the old table. With the corrected table and the old anchors, the whole sky half an hour after sunset drew in the peach meant for the minutes around sunset. The anchors are now the corrected table's values at the same Sun altitudes, so each color stays with its stage of twilight, and a test ties the two together.

The opening view stays 30 minutes after sunset. With the measured sky that view shows 546 visible SBDCs and 2 stars, where it showed 3,290 and 67. An hour after sunset it shows 3,899 and 286. Moving the opening time to 60 minutes would give a fuller first picture. It is left where it is: the opening time is the simulator's own default, the user did not ask for it to change, and the "+60 min" button is one click away. The three place presets are likewise unchanged in time, and two of them, at 30 minutes from sunset or sunrise, now show about half the SBDCs they did.

2026-11-15 — Beta upgrade, unit 4: what "the light of the SBDCs" and "the Moon" mean here

The user asked for "an option to include moonlight and its effects" and to "calculate an approximate ratio of the SBDC light to that of the moon". Unit 4 had to fix what is compared with what.

SBDC light is every sunlit satellite above the horizon, added up. The visible ones alone would leave out most of the light when the satellites are faint, and the question is about light, which the satellites give whether or not each one can be picked out. The total is written as one magnitude.

It is compared with three things. A full Moon overhead, which is the figure a person can picture and does not change with the date. The Moon as set, when it is up, with its phase and its dimming near the horizon. And a moonless natural night sky summed over the hemisphere, about magnitude -6.7 or a thousandth of a lux, because for much of the month there is no Moon and that is the light the satellites add to. All three are ratios of apparent brightness. They are not illuminance on the ground, which would also weight each source by its altitude.

The year moves the Moon and nothing else. The Sun and stars are computed for 2026 throughout the model, and changing that would move every frozen number by a little for no visible gain. The Moon's phase depends on the year completely, so the Moon is placed by its angle from the Sun for the year asked for.

The Moon is off by default. With it off the simulator is as it was. A person who wants the sky of a particular night turns it on.

The readout moved below the sky. It had grown to three lines and covered the horizon. Putting it under the sky costs a strip of sky height and shows the part of the sky where the constellation is densest.

Alternatives considered

OptionConsequence
Total light of all sunlit satellites, against a full Moon, the Moon as set, and a natural night skyThree numbers that answer "how much light is this" on any night. Chosen.
Only the naked-eye-visible satellitesA total that falls to nothing under mitigation while the satellites still light the sky.
Illuminance on the ground, each source weighted by the sine of its altitudeCloser to what a light meter reads, and a ratio that changes with the Moon's altitude in a way a reader has to work out.
A Moon that is always onThe default view would depend on the date's Moon, and every earlier screenshot and count would need one stated.
Add a longitude so that the Moon's place is exactA control the rest of the model has no use for, for a gain of a few degrees.

What this settles, and what it does not

  • The ratios are approximate, as asked. Their largest uncertainty is the satellites' brightness, not the Moon's.
  • Moonlight scattered when the Moon is just below the horizon is not modelled.
  • Unit 5's count of hours uses these totals for one of its three definitions.

2026-11-15 — Beta upgrade, unit 5: what "significant" is counted as

The user asked, with "perhaps", to "calculate the number of hours in a night (or a year) in which the SBDC light is significant at a location". The first entry of this date said three definitions would be reported. This records them as built.

Three rows, and no single answer. At least 100 SBDCs visible to the naked eye; visible SBDCs outnumber visible stars; SBDC light exceeds the sky's own light. Each is a plain statement a person can check against the picture, and they differ by several times for the same setup, which is itself worth seeing.

The third compares with the sky as it is. The first entry worded it as "exceeds the natural moonless night sky" and said moonlight and ambient light would be included. The two only fit together if the comparison is with the sky at that moment: twilight, ambient light and scattered moonlight. Under a dark moonless sky that is the natural night sky.

Both a night and a year. A night is one click and about two seconds; a year is about eight. They are separate buttons so that a person who wants the night does not wait for the year.

The result is computed when asked for, not kept current. Recomputing on every change would cost two seconds per change. A result that no longer matches the settings is dimmed and labelled.

The year is 24 sampled dates. Twice a month takes in the solstices and equinoxes and follows the seasonal change, which is smooth. It samples the Moon's month coarsely.
