Basic Info

  • Population: Galápagos Province had 28,583 residents in Ecuador’s 2022 census; most people live in Puerto Ayora on Santa Cruz, Puerto Baquerizo Moreno on San Cristóbal, and Puerto Villamil on Isabela.
  • Altitude and climate: The inhabited waterfronts are near sea level, while Volcán Wolf reaches 1,707 m (5,600 ft). The equatorial climate has a warmer, wetter season roughly December–May and a cooler, cloudier garúa season roughly June–November. Sea temperatures, visibility, and wildlife activity vary by season and island.
  • Safety: Crime is a low concern on the islands, though petty theft can occur. Secure valuables and use normal precautions in towns, at docks, and on beaches.
  • History: The volcanic islands formed over a hotspot and had no permanent Indigenous settlement. Ecuador annexed the archipelago in 1832; Charles Darwin visited in 1835; Galápagos National Park was established in 1959, and the islands became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1978. Read more on Wikipedia.
  • Protection: Nearly all land outside settled zones belongs to Galápagos National Park, and surrounding waters form a marine reserve. Visitors at protected sites travel with an authorized naturalist guide and follow marked routes and biosecurity rules. Galápagos National Park authority.

Visiting by Boat

Best for range: A multi-night expedition cruise is the practical way to reach remote visitor sites and wake near the next landing. Itineraries are regulated, so compare the actual islands, landing sites, activity level, guide ratio, and vessel—not only the advertised cruise length.

  • Two models: Liveaboard cruises usually run 4–8 days (or combine routes for longer), with cabins, meals, guided shore landings, snorkeling, and transfers bundled. A land-based trip uses hotels on inhabited islands and day boats; it offers more evening freedom but spends more time retracing routes and cannot reach every remote site.
  • Getting aboard: Fly from mainland Ecuador (normally Quito or Guayaquil) to Baltra for Santa Cruz departures or San Cristóbal for Puerto Baquerizo Moreno departures. Some routes begin and end at different airports. Confirm the cruise’s flight instructions before buying airfare; operators commonly coordinate airport-to-vessel transfers.
  • Before departure: Complete the required transit-control and park-entry steps, carry travel insurance that covers evacuation, and follow current luggage/biosecurity restrictions. Fees and procedures can change, so verify them with the Galápagos Governing Council and National Park authority.
  • Choosing a vessel: Smaller yachts can feel intimate and reach the same regulated visitor sites, while larger expedition ships usually provide more deck space, steadier motion, and more specialist staff. Ask about cabin location, stabilizers, wetsuits, snorkeling gear, kayaks/paddleboards, single supplements, guide language, medical support, and what is excluded.
  • Sea conditions: Open-water crossings can be rough, especially in the cooler season. If prone to motion sickness, discuss medication with a clinician before travel and favor a lower, midship cabin on a stabilized vessel.

Best Time for a Week-Plus Cruise

Best overall window: late April through May. This period balances relatively calm seas, warm water, good snorkeling visibility, lush post-rainy-season scenery, blue-footed booby courtship, and the return of waved albatrosses—with generally less holiday pressure than Christmas, New Year, or midsummer.

Galápagos has two broad seasons: a warmer, wetter period from about December–May, usually with calmer water and better visibility, and a cooler June–November period when nutrient-rich upwelling supports intense marine feeding activity. See the Galápagos Conservation Trust climate guide and this seasonal cruise guide.

Choose by priority: Aim for May–July for bird courtship and waved albatrosses on an itinerary that visits Española; August–October for peak marine activity if cold water and rolling seas are acceptable; or October–November for young sea lions. A dedicated scuba liveaboard favors June–November, while a naturalist expedition with hiking and snorkeling favors late April–May.

Route matters at least as much as month: For a trip longer than a week, favor an itinerary that includes western Isabela and Fernandina plus either Española or Genovesa. A route concentrated around the central islands can miss some of the archipelago’s most distinctive wildlife. El Niño and La Niña can disrupt seasonal norms, so recheck conditions before booking.

Attractions

  • Charles Darwin Research Station {531}: Interpretation areas and breeding facilities introduce giant-tortoise conservation and island ecology. Address: Avenida Charles Darwin, Puerto Ayora, Santa Cruz, Galápagos, Ecuador. Hours: daily, typically 7:30–12 and 2–5; confirm locally. Darwin Foundation | Google Maps.
  • Tortuga Bay {648}: A signed trail from Puerto Ayora reaches a long white-sand beach, marine iguanas, and a calmer mangrove lagoon; swimming conditions differ between the exposed and sheltered beaches. Location: Tortuga Bay trailhead, Charles Binford, Puerto Ayora, Santa Cruz. Hours: daily daylight hours; register at the trail entrance. Google Maps.
  • Sierra Negra Volcano {875}: Guided hikes cross Isabela’s humid highlands to the rim of one of the archipelago’s largest calderas; access can close because of volcanic activity or trail conditions. Location: Sierra Negra visitor control, south of Santo Tomás, Isabela, Galápagos. Hours: guided daytime visits by reservation. Google Maps.
  • Galápagos Interpretation Center and Tijeretas {192}: Exhibits explain natural and human history, and adjoining paths lead to viewpoints and a sheltered snorkeling cove. Address: Avenida Alsacio Northia, Puerto Baquerizo Moreno, San Cristóbal, Galápagos. Hours: daily, typically 6–6; confirm locally. Google Maps.
